Chapter 01

WebRTC basics: signaling and NAT traversal

WebRTC lets browsers exchange audio and video directly, but it does not define how they find each other. Exchanging the offer and answer (SDP) is the application's job, and most apps do it over a WebSocket. The caller creates an offer and sends it, the callee replies with an answer, and route candidates (ICE candidates) are sent a few at a time as they are discovered (trickle ICE).[3]

There are three kinds of route candidates: a LAN address (host), your public address as reported by a STUN server (server-reflexive), and a relay through a TURN server (relay). ICE tries every combination and uses the one that connects. Once a route is chosen, keys are exchanged over DTLS and encrypted media starts to flow.[3]

TURN is the last resort when a direct connection fails. Behind a symmetric NAT or a corporate firewall that blocks UDP, traffic goes through TURN. Sources put the share of such calls at "about 10–30%"[3] or "about 18–35% on symmetric NATs and mobile networks"[2], and relaying adds 50–150 ms of latency.[2]

Key point The server only "introduces" peers; media takes a separate path. That division of labour is the foundation of WebRTC. Adding an SFU does not change it. The only difference is that media now passes through a relay server instead of going straight to the other device.
In engawaA Bun WebSocket server handles signaling, and /api/turn-credentials issues short-lived Cloudflare Realtime TURN credentials. Only the server holds the API key. Media never passes through engawa's server.
Chapter 02

The limits of mesh: growth with the square of the headcount

The simplest multi-party call is a full mesh, where everyone connects directly to everyone else. It costs nothing in servers, and with nothing in between, latency is as low as it gets. The problem is the number of streams. With N people there are N(N−1)/2 connections, and each person encodes and sends their video N−1 separate times.[3]

With 6 people each person sends 5 streams; with 10 people it is 9, and the room as a whole carries 10×9 = 90 streams.[3] Home connections have far less upload than download, so the upload runs out first. The sources draw almost the same line: "mesh works up to about 4 people"[4], "it breaks down beyond about 4"[2], "2–5 people".[1]

Besides the stream count, what bites in practice is one-sided failure. A mesh runs ICE separately for every pair, so "A–B and B–C connect, but A–C does not" can happen. The more people, the more pairs, and the higher the chance that some pair is missing.

The deciding factor for #310 was the one-sided failure described above.
Chapter 03

The MCU detour

The next idea after mesh is the MCU (Multipoint Control Unit). The server decodes everyone's video, composites it into a single picture, re-encodes it, and sends each person just one stream. From the device's point of view it is one stream up and one down, which is very light.

In exchange, the server gets heavy. Decoding and re-encoding N streams in real time is said to take roughly 4–10× the compute of an SFU for the same number of people.[4] The processing adds latency, and the layout is fixed by the server.[1] MCUs are still chosen when you want a single combined recording, need to bridge to SIP or the phone network, or have devices too weak to receive more than one stream.[4]

Key point In a virtual office, everyone arranges other people's tiles at whatever size they like on their own screen. Keeping the layout on each device is a big reason to choose an SFU over an MCU.
Chapter 04

How an SFU works: send one stream, let the server choose

An SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit) is a relay server that forwards incoming packets, without decoding them, to the recipients who need them. Specifications also call it an SFM (Selective Forwarding Middlebox).[1] Each participant is a publisher that sends its own media once, and at the same time a subscriber that picks which other people's media to receive.

This makes bandwidth asymmetric. Upload stays at one stream regardless of headcount, while download grows with the number of people you want to see.[1] Upload per device is O(1); SFU egress is O(N). As a concrete estimate, 10 people at 720p with three simulcast layers come out at about 3 Mbps of upload per device and about 27 Mbps of SFU egress.[2] On home connections the upload is the thin pipe, so the SFU approach is to keep that thin side constant and absorb headcount with the fatter download and data-centre bandwidth.

Isolating one person's weakness from everyone

In a mesh you encode separately for each peer, but if the app sets one quality ceiling for everyone, it ends up lowering everyone's quality to suit the thinnest peer. Per-peer ceilings avoid that, at the cost of more sender processing and upload. With an SFU in between, the sender only sends to the SFU, and each receiver's circumstances can be absorbed between the SFU and that receiver. The technique that makes this concrete is simulcast, covered in the next chapter.

SFU topologies

  • Single SFU: simple to build, but quality drops for distant participants and the limit of one machine becomes the limit of the room.[1]
  • Cascading: several SFUs are linked and each person connects to a nearby one. It scales to tens of thousands and beyond, but is harder to build.[1]
  • Publisher SFU: senders publish to a nearby SFU and receivers pull from the sender's SFU. Relatively simple to build, but harder to monitor and control.[1]

What matters if you run an SFU yourself

In the community around mediasoup, an open-source SFU, the bottleneck is said to be CPU rather than bandwidth. You start one worker per CPU core and assign calls to workers. The heavy objects are consumers, each representing the forwarding of one stream to one receiver, and the official guideline is about 500 per worker. When everyone sends and receives audio and video, there are about 2n(n−1) consumers: 24 for 4 people, 112 for 8, and about 480 for 16.[5] Practitioners also report that pausing unused consumers instead of destroying them stops RTP from flowing and saves CPU.[5]

In engawaengawa uses Cloudflare Realtime SFU and does not run its own. Worker allocation and cascading are Cloudflare's job. engawa's server only proxies the control API at /api/sfu/*, and only the server holds the App ID and Token. The forwarded paths are restricted by an allowlist (SSRF protection).
Chapter 05

Simulcast and SVC: the heart of simultaneous streaming

An SFU only forwards, so it cannot re-create a different quality for each receiver. Instead, the sender sends several qualities at once from the start, and the SFU picks which one each receiver gets. That is simulcast. One example given is three layers: 1080p@4Mbps, 720p@2Mbps and 360p@500kbps.[3] Up to three layers is typical.[1]

SVC: layers inside a single stream

SVC (Scalable Video Coding) stacks resolution and frame-rate layers inside one stream, and the SFU drops the layers each receiver does not need. VP9, AV1 and H.265 support it.[3] Using only frame-rate layers, called "temporal scalability", lets the SFU hand a thinned-out frame rate to weak receivers.[1] One source notes that at 100–1,000 people SVC is more bandwidth-efficient than simulcast, and that handling many simulcast layers on an SFU hits CPU limits before bandwidth.[2]

Chapter 06

Bandwidth estimation and congestion control: deciding how much to send

To decide which layers to send and receive, you need to know how much headroom the connection has. WebRTC's standard mechanism is Google Congestion Control (GCC). The receiver tracks changes in packet inter-arrival time (the delay gradient) with a Kalman filter and treats growing delay as congestion. The result is reported back to the sender via REMB or transport-cc (TWCC).[3]

When it detects congestion, the sender slows down by about 8%; when things are stable, it probes for headroom by increasing about 5% every 1.5 seconds or so.[3] It starts conservatively at around 300 kbps, so video tends to look rough for the first few seconds of a call. Supplying an initial bitrate hint can mitigate this.[2]

With an SFU, the important point is that this estimation is split per leg. Congestion is measured separately between the sender and the SFU and between the SFU and each receiver. The SFU can look at each receiver's estimate and pick a layer that fits. This is what actually makes "one person's thin download doesn't affect everyone" true.

In engawa On top of the browser's built-in GCC, the app scores the connection every 5 seconds and puts it into one of four tiers, 0–3 (#189). The inputs are loss rate (2 / 5 / 10%), RTT (250 / 500 / 800 ms) and estimated upload (below 900 / 500 / 250 kbps). The tier moves down one step after 2 consecutive bad readings and up one step after 6 consecutive good ones (it used to jump straight to tier 3; fixed in #274). Chapter 07

Loss and jitter: filling in what didn't arrive

On the internet, packets get dropped and their arrival times wobble. There are three broad countermeasures.[3]

TechniqueHow it worksCostSuited to
FECSend redundant data up front and reconstruct anything missingAbout 5–10% more bandwidthRandom loss
NACKReport missing sequence numbers and get them resentOne round trip of delayBurst loss
Jitter bufferThe receiver holds a little before playingLatency grows by the amount heldArrival-time variation

The Opus audio codec has its own FEC (in-band FEC), PLC to fill in missing audio by guesswork, and DTX to nearly stop sending during silence.[3] Audio intelligibility is said to start suffering above 2% loss, and above 5% FEC can no longer keep up.[2] Jitter buffers are typically around 150–200 ms[3], and a common target is keeping p95 jitter under 30 ms.[2]

For video, loss handling ties into the quality layers. If loss persists, sending a lower layer to cut the volume works better than retransmitting piece by piece. In real designs, bandwidth estimation, layer selection and loss handling are therefore tuned together.

In engawa Opus is rewritten for low latency (rtc/sdp.ts): 20 ms packets (ptime=20), in-band FEC on, and DTX off (to avoid losing the first few frames when someone starts talking). From tier 2 up, audio is sent twice with Opus RED. The jitter buffer starts at a low-latency 100 ms, widens to 150 / 220 / 300 ms according to measured jitter, and shrinks back once things calm down.
Chapter 08

Audio: delivery and noise suppression

Audio is a small stream of a few dozen kbps, yet it is the first thing to ruin how a conversation feels. You can keep talking through a moment of frozen video, but when voices drop out or lag, the conversation itself stops. Audio design therefore puts latency and resistance to gaps ahead of bandwidth. This chapter looks first at getting audio to the other side (delivery), then at removing noise before it is sent (noise suppression).

Delivery: configuring Opus

WebRTC audio is almost always Opus. Opus varies from about 6 kbps for speech to about 64 kbps for music, and has its own mechanisms for coping with loss.[3]

MechanismWhat it doesCost
in-band FECEach packet carries a low-quality copy of the previous frame, so a single lost packet can be recovered from the nextSlightly more bandwidth
REDRe-sends the previous packet itself alongside the current one; survives consecutive lossesRoughly double the audio bandwidth
PLCFills a missing stretch by extrapolating from what came before and afterSounds unnatural if the gap is long
DTXNearly stops sending during silenceThe first few frames of speech can be lost

The standard packet length (ptime) is 20 ms. Shorter packets cut latency but raise the share of headers and reduce efficiency. As for loss tolerance, the rule of thumb is that intelligibility suffers above 2% loss and FEC can't keep up above 5%.[2] On connections worse than that, you give bandwidth to audio even if it means cutting video.

Delivery: putting audio first

  • Send priority: set send priorities so that under congestion video is cut first and audio is protected to the end.
  • Send mono: stereo doubles the bandwidth without making speech any easier to understand.
  • Forward without mixing: unlike an MCU, an SFU forwards audio one person at a time without mixing, and the receiver's browser overlays them on playback. Server load stays small, but the download carries one audio stream per person. For large rooms, receiving only the people who are speaking is recommended.[5]
  • Start with a short jitter buffer: for audio, latency directly sets the rhythm of a conversation. Keep the buffer short while jitter is low and widen it only when jitter grows.

Noise suppression: classic and neural

Classic noise suppression is built from three stages: deciding whether someone is speaking (VAD), estimating the noise spectrum, and subtracting it. It was hard to tune. RNNoise keeps that signal-processing structure but has a small neural network learn only the parts that are hard to tune.[7]

  • It splits audio into 22 frequency bands, and the network outputs a gain between 0 and 1 for each band
  • Noise left between the bands is removed with a filter tuned to the voice's pitch
  • The model is small: 3 GRU layers, about 85 kB with 8-bit weights
  • Even the unoptimised C implementation runs about 60× faster than real time on an x86 CPU
  • As a by-product it also outputs the probability that the frame is speech (VAD)

On the commercial side, Krisp is widely used. Gather runs the Krisp SDK in a separate process so its failures can't spread, and adds automatic gain control (AGC) afterwards to even out volume.[6] Browsers also ship echo cancellation (AEC), noise suppression (NS) and AGC, which you can enable through getUserMedia constraints.

Noise suppression: meeting the deadline

Audio processing has to finish each 10 ms frame before the next one arrives. Missing the deadline even once causes dropouts or distortion. Gather moved its audio processing from JavaScript to C++ to avoid garbage-collection pauses, and Windows throttling the app as a background process was another cause of missed deadlines.[6] In the browser, the AudioWorklet, which runs on a dedicated thread separate from the main thread, plays this role. Even when rendering or JavaScript gets heavy, audio processing keeps running.

Noise suppression: gating what can't be removed

Neural noise suppression can remove steady noise such as air conditioning or keyboards. Sudden sounds — a passing motorbike, birdsong, household noise — tend to slip through. So it is paired with a voice gate that silences the microphone completely while you are not speaking. The VAD that RNNoise already outputs can drive the decision, so no extra model is needed.

In engawa Delivery: Opus is mono, 20 ms, with in-band FEC on and DTX off, and RED is added when the connection degrades (tier 2 and up). On the mesh path audio is capped at 64 kbps. On the SFU path audio has the highest send priority, and video is cut first under congestion. On reconnect, everyone's microphones are received first in one batch, and at tier 3 video reception stops so the whole download goes to audio.

Noise suppression (on by default): audio first goes through the browser's built-in echo cancellation, noise suppression and AGC, then through RNNoise (WASM) running in engawa's own AudioWorklet. RNNoise's output then goes through an 8 kHz low-pass (4th-order Butterworth, 24 dB/octave): on some PCs the built-in mic picks up a high-pitched electrical whine (coil whine) that slips through the gate along with the voice as a glassy ringing. Everything up to 8 kHz is the HD voice (wideband) band, so the voice barely changes. The voice gate is configured as follows.
  • Opens when VAD reaches 0.6 and closes below 0.3 (two thresholds prevent flapping)
  • Stays open for 300 ms after the close condition is met (so word endings and short pauses aren't cut)
  • Fades in over 5 ms when opening and out over 60 ms when closing (no clicks)
  • Looks two frames ahead before deciding to open or close (so word onsets aren't lost). Total latency is 30 ms as a result
If the WASM hasn't loaded within 5 seconds, engawa falls back to the raw, unprocessed microphone. The model and the audio never leave the device (no CDN either).
Chapter 09

Screen sharing: protect the text, not the motion

Screen sharing is also a video stream, but what it needs to protect is the opposite of a camera. For a camera, smooth facial motion matters and a little blur goes unnoticed. For screen sharing, what matters is that small text on a slide or a single character of code stays legible. There is little motion, and most of the screen is unchanged from the previous frame. But scrolling or switching pages changes the whole screen at once, and for that moment a lot of bandwidth is needed.

CameraScreen share
What to protectSmooth motionSharp text and thin lines
What to cut under congestionResolutionFrame rate
Bandwidth patternRoughly constantUsually small, spikes on changes
Send in layers?Simulcast helpsLower layers make text unreadable, so they're useless

Telling the encoder what it is looking at

Unless it knows what is being captured, the browser's encoder makes camera-style decisions. The video track's contentHint tells it. The W3C specification recommends the following handling for each value.[8]

contentHintSuited toUnder congestion (degradationPreference)
motionCameras, video, gamesmaintain-framerate: keep it smooth even at lower resolution
detailSlides, text-heavy web pages, diagramsmaintain-resolution: keep resolution even if frames drop
textMostly text, with sharp edgesmaintain-resolution. AV1 uses its text-oriented coding tools

If the app explicitly sets the degradationPreference send parameter, that takes precedence.[8] The specification also notes that even text does not guarantee the text will be readable.[8]

Limit at capture time

Capturing a 4K or Retina display as-is means paying for capture and downscaling on every frame, only to shrink it before sending anyway. Capping resolution and frame rate in the getDisplayMedia capture constraints from the start saves the sender's CPU.

One stream, no simulcast

A screen share as small as the camera's q layer crushes text into illegibility. Splitting it into layers gives receivers no meaningful choice, so the basic approach is to send screen sharing as a single higher-bitrate stream and, under congestion, drop frame rate to keep resolution. There is also the practical point that a meeting can't move forward unless everyone can read the same document.

Don't break starting and stopping

  • Don't rebuild the path when restarting: recreating the send slot (transceiver) every time sharing stops and restarts makes renegotiation prone to failure. Keep the slot when stopping, and put the new track back into the same slot when restarting.
  • Handle the browser's or OS's "Stop sharing": users can stop sharing without the app's button, so run the same cleanup on the track's ended event.
  • Mind the order of user gestures: some APIs, like getDisplayMedia, can only be called right after a user click. Calling another such API first in the same click can make sharing itself fail.
In engawa
  • Capture: limited to 1920px on the long edge and 15 fps, with no audio captured. The track gets contentHint = 'detail'.
  • Sending: degradationPreference is maintain-resolution, so frame rate is cut first under congestion. On the SFU path it is not simulcast and goes out as one stream at 2 Mbps, 1920px on the long edge, 15 fps (#325). On the mesh path it is 3 Mbps with fewer than 3 peers and 1.5 Mbps otherwise, dropping to 10 fps and 1280px at 8 or more. The mesh path lowers it further by connection tier (70% at tier 1; 800 kbps, 8 fps, 1280px at tier 2; 500 kbps, 5 fps at tier 3). The SFU path skips these caps and leaves it to the browser's bandwidth estimation.
  • Start and stop: a bug where turning sharing off and on again stopped it reaching others was fixed by reusing the SFU transceiver (#151). The OS's "Stop sharing" runs the same cleanup as the toolbar button.
  • Viewing: while sharing, other people's face tiles can be shown in an always-on-top mini window (Document Picture-in-Picture) (#318, experimental). The API that opens that window also consumes the click's permission, so it is always opened after getDisplayMedia.
  • Recording: text in recorded screen shares was unreadable; this was fixed by compositing at a fixed 2× resolution with high-quality downscaling (#292).
Chapter 10

Receivers choose: don't receive what you're not watching

Even with an SFU, download grows with the number of people you want to see. The best way to keep download down in a large room is to not receive what you're not looking at. The mediasoup community also recommends not receiving everyone's audio and video at once, but limiting the number of simultaneous speakers and paging the display.[5] For 50–200 people, a setup centred on receiving the active speakers is recommended.[4]

  • Pick the layer by tile size: a small tile doesn't need a high layer.
  • Drop off-screen and background-tab tiles to the lowest layer, or stop them: quality doesn't matter for what can't be seen.
  • Start low right after joining: if high layers from everyone arrive the moment you enter a large room, the download chokes.
  • Receive audio first: audio is what makes a connection feel connected.
In engawaAll four are implemented: layer selection by tile width and dropping off-screen and background-tab tiles to q (#270), starting at q in meeting rooms (#339), and on reconnect receiving everyone's microphones first in a single request before video (#256). At tier 3 video reception stops and the download is used for audio only.
Chapter 11

Recovering from failure: confine it to one person

Even with bandwidth and loss handled, connections can still drop. The practical pitfalls the sources list are these.[2]

  • If you don't call restartIce() when switching from Wi-Fi to mobile data, the call can drop after about 30 seconds
  • If the path MTU is below 1280 bytes, the DTLS handshake can fail silently, so pin it to 1200 bytes
  • If you don't call pc.close() on exit, TURN allocations linger and run up the bill
  • TURN takes 1.5–3 seconds to start, so keep 2–3 warm spares ready

SFUs have a weakness of their own: each person carries all sending and receiving on a single connection to the SFU. One connection makes things stable, but rebuilding that one connection means rebuilding sending and receiving with everyone. If you rebuild your own connection because someone else is having trouble, that triggers others to redo their receiving, and it cascades.

Key point For an SFU to truly mean "one person's trouble doesn't affect everyone", you need failure isolation as well as bandwidth isolation (simulcast and layer selection). Failing to receive one peer must not be treated as a failure of your whole connection.
In engawa This failure isolation was added step by step. A rejected subscription to one peer became a per-peer retry (#251). When the SFU fails, engawa doesn't fall back to mesh; it rebuilds at intervals of 0→2→5→10→20→30 seconds while keeping the tiles on screen (#256). Failures when re-receiving were confined to per-peer retries, stopping a cascade of everyone reconnecting (#332). While other people's media is still arriving, your own connection is treated as healthy and not rebuilt (#342). Outside the media connection, a ping every 5 seconds and a 12-second silence timeout detect half-dead WebSockets, which reconnect indefinitely (#189).

Chapter 13

The Gather case: rebuilding the audio pipeline

Gather, a service of the same kind, publishes very little on its engineering blog. The one technical article we found is a retrospective on audio problems after the Gather 2.0 release.[6] It says nothing about media routing such as SFU versus P2P; it is about on-device audio processing.

  • Cause: after a Windows 11 update, Gather was treated as a background app and throttled, so audio processing missed its deadlines. They fixed it by securing audio-thread priority with Windows' audio scheduling service (MMCSS).
  • From JavaScript to C++: to avoid garbage-collection pauses, audio processing moved to C++ and runs in a separate Electron process.
  • Isolating noise suppression: the Krisp SDK was moved to its own process so its failures can't spread, and automatic gain control (AGC) was added afterwards.
  • Measurement: after each call users rate the audio from 1 to 5 stars, and Gather tracked the share of low ratings. It reports a 50% drop since the release (Gather's own figure).
  • Caveat: the person reporting a problem isn't always the person with the performance problem. For example, reports from Mac users turned out to be problems on the Windows side of the person they were talking to.
Key point Even with correct routing, audio falls apart if on-device processing misses its deadline. And "whose audio is bad" can't be told from the listener's impression alone. The lesson of Gather's article is to collect both network metrics and perceived-quality ratings, and not to judge from just one of them.
In engawaNoise suppression runs RNNoise (WASM) in engawa's own AudioWorklet, which follows the same direction as Gather: keep it off the main thread. To show whose connection is at fault, tiles display why video stalled ("your connection", "their connection", "switching quality") (#313). Perceived-quality ratings (stars) are not collected yet.

Open issues

On the mesh path, connection quality is judged by the worst of all connections, so one peer's bad connection lowers all of your sending. This doesn't show up in the default setup (SFU for 2 or more), but it happens when the SFU isn't configured or SFU_PROMOTE_AT is raised. It is tracked in iitenkida7/engawa#349.

A possible next step, following Gather's article, is to collect perceived quality with a short post-call rating. Together with the counts of sfu-rebuild and video-stall in the netlog, that would show whether the 10/16 fixes are actually working.
